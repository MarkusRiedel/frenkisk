# Frenkisk — four texts

This is what **Frenkisk** looks like: a reconstruction of the Merovingian
Frankish spoken around 550.

⚠ **These are not showcase pieces built for the occasion.** Each was
produced by **two translators working separately**, who had nothing but
the three delivered files — the grammar, the dictionary, the index — and
were forbidden to open anything else or to draw on Old Saxon. They did
not know each other and never compared their work.

**What follows is what both arrived at**, word for word. Where they
diverged, it is said.

⚠⚠ **The source texts were given to the translators in French**, and the
project was built as a translation target from French. The originals are
rendered into English below so that this page can be read without it —
but what the two readers actually worked from was the French. Saying
otherwise would claim an experiment that never took place.

---

## I. A saying

> The smith chooses his iron; the judge chooses his words.
> — Is the word heavier than the iron, then?
> — It is heavier. Iron breaks, and one sees that it has broken. A word
> breaks, and nobody sees it.

> **Smith sīn īsarn kiusid, raginburgio sīna word kiusid.**
> **— Ist thanne word swāriro than īsarn ?**
> **— It ist swāriro. Īsarn wirthid gabrokan, endi man sihid, that it
> gabrokan ist. Word wirthid gabrokan, endi ni sihid it neoman.**

*Identical in both, down to one comma.*

⚠ The language separates here what the source ran together: `wirthid
gabrokan` states the **process** — "is in the act of breaking" — and
`gabrokan ist` the **resulting state**. The original used one word for
both.

## II. An interrogation

> — Who are you? Where do you come from?
> — I am Gundulf's son. I come from the north.
> — Why are you here?
> — Because my brother drove me out.
> — When?
> — At our father's death.
> — What do you want from us?
> — Nothing. Water, and the road.

> **— Hwē bist thū ? Hwār fon quimis thū ?**
> **— Ik bim Gundulfas sunu. Ik quimu fon northē.**
> **— Hwī bist thū hēr ?**
> **— Hwanda mīn brōther beut mik.**
> **— Hwanne ?**
> **— Thō unser fader starf.**
> **— Hwat wilis thū fon uns ?**
> **— Niowiht. Watar endi weg.**

*Seven sentences out of eight identical — the best convergence obtained.*

⚠ **"At our father's death" has no nominal translation.** Both wrote
`Thō unser fader starf` — "when our father died". Germanic prefers the
subordinate clause to the prepositional phrase, and a word-for-word copy
of the original would have been refused by the grammar.

⚠ Their only divergence was the position of the verb after `hwanda`
"because". It has since been ruled on.

## III. An irrealis

> If I had known, I would not have come. The old dog would still be
> alive, and the house would still be standing.
> — You could not have known.
> — I could. I had been told twice.

> **Of ik wissi, ni quāmi ik. Aldo hund wāri noh kwik, endi that hūs noh
> stōdi.**
> **— Ni mahtes thū witan.**
> **— Ik mahta. Man habda it mī twā stundā gasagd.**

⚠ **The language does not distinguish "I would not come" from "I would
not have come".** A single preterite subjunctive carries both, just as a
single preterite carries the imperfect and the simple past. This is not a
gap in the reconstruction: it is the state of Germanic at this date.

⚠ **The two diverged on "I would not have come"**, and both declared it:
`ni quāmi ik` — the plain preterite subjunctive, given here — and
`ni wāri ik kuman`, the periphrasis. **The grammar holds them
equivalent.**

⚠ `Aldo hund` — the weak adjective **with no article** is enough to say
"the old dog, that one". And it is the adjective that carries the gender:
`hund` alone is ambiguous, since the word means **both "dog" and
"hundred"**. The homonymy is Germanic itself.

## IV. A hall

> The hall is long and dark. The stones at the far end are grey, those by
> the door whiter. A cold fire, an empty table, three narrow benches.
> Nobody has taken the spear since the king died.
> It is a fine hall. It smells of smoke and iron.

> **Seli ist lang endi thiustri. Stainā thes endas sint grīsa, stainā
> thero duri sint hwītiro. Kald fiur, īdal beud, thrī enga bankā.**
> **Ni hebid it neoman ganoman, sīthor the kuning starf.**
> **That ist skauni seli. Thār ist stank raukas endi īsarnas.**

*Six sentences identical in both.*

⚠ **`Kald fiur, īdal beud, thrī enga bankā`** — no "and" before the last
term. The pattern "A, B **and** C" is a habit of the source language;
Germanic strings a series together without a coordinator.

⚠ **"It smells of smoke" has no verb.** The language has no verb of
smelling at all — only the noun `stank` "smell". Both translators wrote
`Thār ist stank raukas` "there is a smell of smoke", and both reported
the loss. **The source has a verb where Frenkisk has a noun, and it is
Frenkisk that decides.**

## What these texts prove, and what they do not

**They prove the language is usable.** Two separate readers, with no
resource but three files, produce the same text — often word for word —
and themselves declare the places where the documentation falls silent.
No form was invented in these four texts.

**They do not prove that this is Frankish.** Real Frankish is almost
entirely lost: what survives is latinised proper names, the glosses of
the Salic law, and the words it left to French — *guerre*, *garder*,
*heaume*, *bleu*, *franc*. Frenkisk fills its gaps **under discipline** —
a declared hierarchy of witnesses, dated rulings, open cases left open
rather than quietly filled — **but it does fill them.** This is a
historically anchored conlang, not a scholarly reconstruction.

The places where it had to choose are written out, one by one, in
[`STATE.md`](STATE.md).
